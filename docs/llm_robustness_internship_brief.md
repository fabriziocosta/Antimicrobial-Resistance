# How Robust Are LLM-Based Scientific Conclusions?

The central aim of this project is to test whether scientific interest in the biomedical literature can be modelled as an early warning signal for antimicrobial resistance. If research attention toward a pathogen-antibiotic pair is rising faster than expected, can that help identify where future resistance problems may be more likely to emerge, and where surveillance should concentrate? The student choosing this project will implement a complete mini-pipeline from scratch that follows this logic: collect a sample of PubMed abstracts for pathogen-antibiotic pairs, use LLMs to decide which papers contain relevant evidence for the target pair, aggregate the accepted papers into yearly scientific-interest statistics, and use those statistics to highlight pairs that may deserve closer monitoring.

The robustness question is central because the LLM is part of the measurement process. If a different model, prompt setting, temperature, or cost tier changes which abstracts are counted, it may also change the estimated scientific-interest trend, the ranking of pathogen-antibiotic pairs, and the practical conclusion about where surveillance attention should go. The project will therefore not only build a scientific-interest pipeline, but also stress-test it: do different LLMs lead to the same high-priority pairs, or would the choice of model change the conclusions about where future AMR problems are more likely to arise?

The work has several key elements. First, the student will define a manageable evaluation dataset: a selected set of pathogen-antibiotic pairs, publication years, titles, and abstracts, ideally including pairs with known AMR relevance as well as ambiguous or weak co-occurrence examples. Second, they will design a clear LLM classification task: for each abstract, the model should judge only the specified pathogen-antibiotic pair and return a structured decision such as `yes`, `no`, or `uncertain`, plus optional confidence, rationale, and evidence phrases. Third, they will aggregate the accepted decisions into yearly publication counts and simple scientific-interest measures, for example by identifying pairs whose AMR-related literature is increasing relative to their previous trajectory or broader background attention. Fourth, they will rank pairs by rising scientific interest and interpret what those rankings might imply for AMR surveillance prioritisation. Fifth, they will implement the robustness layer: running the same cases through multiple LLMs or settings, comparing agreement rates, disagreement examples, stability of yearly counts, stability of rankings, and cost per usable classification. The final interpretation should answer whether cheap models are good enough, which cases are most fragile, how much aggregation smooths out individual errors, and whether the surveillance-prioritisation conclusions are stable.

This is a strong choice for a student who wants a project with clear real-world relevance beyond antimicrobial resistance. The AMR question is important in its own right: surveillance resources are limited, and any scalable signal that helps identify pathogen-antibiotic combinations needing closer attention could be valuable for research prioritisation and public health planning. At the same time, the technical pattern is widely transferable. Many organisations are now adding LLMs to research, policy, healthcare, finance, legal, and business analytics workflows, but they still need to know whether the outputs are stable, auditable, cost-effective, and safe to build decisions on. Compared with a purely domain-specific analysis, this project gives the student a chance to build an end-to-end evaluation system and develop broadly useful skills in data collection, prompt design, structured LLM outputs, time-series summarisation, reproducible analysis, uncertainty assessment, visualisation, and cost-performance trade-off analysis.
